## Response (verbatim)

No, intermittent fasting has not been proven to extend human lifespan. The honest answer is that we don't have the evidence we'd need to make that claim, and it's unlikely we'll get it any time soon. Demonstrating a lifespan effect in humans would require randomized controlled trials lasting decades, with thousands of participants maintaining a dietary protocol consistently across most of their adult lives. No such trial exists, and the practical and ethical obstacles to running one are formidable.

What we do have is a layered body of evidence that gestures in interesting directions without closing the question. In short-lived model organisms — yeast, worms, flies, and rodents — caloric restriction and various fasting protocols reliably extend lifespan, sometimes dramatically. The effect is one of the most robust findings in biogerontology. However, the magnitude shrinks as you move up the evolutionary tree. The two long-running primate studies of caloric restriction in rhesus monkeys (at Wisconsin and the NIA) produced mixed results: the Wisconsin study found a survival benefit, the NIA study did not, and the differences seem to trace to diet composition and genetics rather than calories per se.

In humans, the evidence is mostly about surrogate markers rather than mortality. Trials like CALERIE-2, which tested sustained caloric restriction (not strictly fasting) in healthy adults for two years, showed improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, inflammatory markers, and some indicators of biological aging. Intermittent fasting protocols specifically — time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, the 5:2 — have been studied in shorter trials and tend to produce weight loss and metabolic improvements roughly comparable to ordinary calorie restriction, though some recent trials (like the ones from Krista Varady's group and a notable JAMA Internal Medicine trial of time-restricted eating) suggest the advantages over plain calorie counting are modest or absent.

The leap from "improves metabolic markers" to "extends lifespan" is exactly the kind of inference epidemiology has historically gotten wrong. Plenty of interventions improve biomarkers without translating into longer lives, and some — hormone replacement therapy in the 1990s being a classic example — turn out to have the opposite effect once long-term outcomes are measured. So while it's reasonable to suspect that something like fasting might confer longevity benefits, calling it "proven" overstates what the evidence supports.

There are also reasons for caution. Intermittent fasting can be hard to sustain, may worsen disordered eating patterns in vulnerable people, and a 2024 observational study presented at an American Heart Association meeting raised (contested) concerns about cardiovascular mortality with very narrow eating windows. Observational data on this question is particularly weak because people who fast tend to differ systematically from those who don't in ways that are hard to control for.

The defensible summary is something like: intermittent fasting appears reasonably safe for most healthy adults, produces modest metabolic and weight benefits, and is biologically plausible as a longevity intervention based on animal data and mechanistic studies of pathways like autophagy, mTOR, and insulin signaling. But "biologically plausible and supported by animal models" is a much weaker claim than "proven to extend human lifespan," and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something — often literally.
