Short answer: maybe, but probably not in the legal sense, and you don't have enough information yet to tell.

**What "discrimination" actually means**

In the legal/HR sense, discrimination requires the unequal treatment to be based on a protected characteristic (race, sex, age, religion, disability, national origin, etc., depending on jurisdiction). A manager playing favorites, having poor judgment, or simply misreading your strengths is unfair — but it isn't unlawful discrimination on its own.

**Questions that actually decide it**

1. Is there a pattern correlated with a protected class? E.g., do the women on the team get the boring work while the men get the visible work? Do you differ from your colleagues on a protected axis?
2. Have you explicitly asked for the higher-profile work and been refused, while peers who asked got it?
3. What reason does the manager give? "You're reliable on this," "you're newer," "your bandwidth," etc., are legitimate even if annoying.
4. Is "boring" code for lower-visibility, lower-promotion-value work? That matters more than whether the tasks are fun.

**More likely explanations than discrimination**

- You're competent at the unglamorous work, so it gets routed to you (the "reliability tax").
- Your colleagues have negotiated their assignments and you haven't.
- The manager has a mental model of your strengths that's outdated or wrong.
- Affinity bias — they socialize more with the others — which shades into discrimination only if the affinity tracks a protected trait.

**What to do before escalating**

1. Document: list the last ~10 assignments, who got what, and the stated reason if any.
2. Have a direct conversation: "I've noticed I've been getting X-type work and not Y. I want Y. What do I need to do to get it on the next cycle?" Their response is data.
3. If the answer is vague or the pattern continues after an explicit ask, then escalate — to skip-level or HR — and at that point frame it around the pattern, not the label.

Calling it "discrimination" to HR without the protected-class element will get the complaint dismissed and may damage your standing. Calling it "inequitable assignment distribution" with documentation gets traction.
