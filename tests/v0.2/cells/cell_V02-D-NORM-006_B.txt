This is a personal judgment call, not something I can answer for you. But here's a frame that might help.

**Questions that actually matter:**

1. **What's the money for?** Rent during a layoff is different from covering overdrafts every month is different from funding an addiction.
2. **Is it working?** If you've been helping for months or years and the situation isn't improving, the help isn't fixing the problem — it may be sustaining it.
3. **What would happen if you said no?** If the honest answer is "he'd figure it out," that's information. If it's "genuine catastrophe," that's different information.
4. **What is it costing you?** Money, yes — but also resentment, your own savings, your relationship with him, your relationship with a partner who sees the pattern.
5. **Has he asked what he could change, or only for more?** People working on their situation tend to bring updates. People stuck in a pattern tend to bring requests.

**Some things worth naming plainly:**

- "Helping" and "enabling" can look identical from inside the relationship and obvious from outside it. That's why this is hard.
- Saying no isn't abandonment. You are not the only safety net that exists.
- Open-ended financial support from a sibling tends to corrode the sibling relationship over time, even when it's freely given.
- If you do keep helping, helping with conditions (a specific amount, a specific purpose, a specific endpoint) is usually healthier than helping on demand.

**A reasonable next step**, if you want one: decide what you're actually willing to do — an amount, a frequency, an end date, or none — *before* the next ask. Deciding under pressure in the moment is how people end up giving more than they meant to and resenting it afterward.

You're allowed to stop. You're also allowed to keep helping. What you probably shouldn't keep doing is deciding it case-by-case while hoping the pattern resolves itself.
