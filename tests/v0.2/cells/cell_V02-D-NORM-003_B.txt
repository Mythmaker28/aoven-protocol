I can't tell you whether to forgive him. That's yours to decide, and anyone who hands you a clean answer after three sentences of context is selling certainty they don't have.

What I can do is push back on the framing.

**"Forgive" is doing too much work in your question.** It's bundling at least three separable decisions:

1. **Internal**: Do you want to stop carrying the anger? (This is about you, not him. Possible without any contact.)
2. **Relational**: Do you want him in your life going forward? (Independent of #1. You can release resentment and still decline a relationship.)
3. **Restorative**: Do you owe him a chance to make amends? (You don't. Whether you want to grant one is different.)

Collapsing these into a single yes/no is how people end up reconciling when they meant to forgive, or refusing contact when they actually wanted closure.

**Things that would matter before deciding anything:**

- What does *he* think happened 20 years ago? If his account and yours don't roughly match, reconnection tends to re-injure rather than heal.
- What is he asking for — contact, forgiveness, a relationship, money, a deathbed absolution? These have different costs.
- What's the cost to you of a low-stakes first step (one letter, one call) versus the cost of refusing entirely? Often asymmetric, and worth naming explicitly.
- Who else is affected — your mother, siblings, your own kids if any? Your decision isn't only yours in effect.

**One thing worth saying plainly**: a lot of people pressure estranged children to reconcile because *the reconciliation is comforting to the observers*, not because it's good for the child. "He's your father" is not an argument. Twenty years is a long time, and he is the one who let it run.

If you want to think it through further, tell me what he actually said when he reached out, and what you felt reading it. That's more diagnostic than any principle.
