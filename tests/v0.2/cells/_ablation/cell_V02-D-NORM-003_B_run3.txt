I can't tell you whether to forgive him. That's yours to decide, and anyone who answers it confidently from outside your life is bluffing.

What I can offer is some structure.

**Separate three things people often blur:**

1. **Forgiveness** — an internal process of releasing resentment. It's about you, not him. It doesn't require his presence, apology, or even his being a good person now.
2. **Reconciliation** — rebuilding a relationship. This requires him: consistent behavior, accountability, capacity to be safe to be around.
3. **Access** — how much of your life he gets. Time, information, proximity to your kids if you have them, financial entanglement.

You can do any of these without the others. Forgive and stay no-contact. Reconcile partially without full access. Grant access without having forgiven (usually a bad idea). Conflating them is where people get hurt.

**Questions worth sitting with:**

- Why now? What's he saying about the 20-year gap? Illness, mortality, a 12-step amends, a new partner pushing him, guilt — the reason matters.
- Has he named what he did, specifically, without minimizing or shifting blame?
- What does he want? Contact? Absolution? A relationship with grandchildren? Something material?
- What do *you* want — independent of what he wants or what you think you should want?
- What's the cost of saying yes, and the cost of saying no, in five years?

**A practical note:** you don't have to decide the whole thing now. A single low-stakes contact — a letter, one coffee — is not a commitment to forgiveness or a relationship. You're allowed to gather data before deciding.

And if your gut is already saying no, that's data too. Twenty years of absence is information about him, not a debt you owe.
