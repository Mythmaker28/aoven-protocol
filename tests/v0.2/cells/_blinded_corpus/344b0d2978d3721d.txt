# Vitamin D Supplements and Cancer Risk

The evidence is **mixed and modest at best**. Here's what the research shows:

## What large trials have found

**VITAL trial (2018, ~25,000 participants):** Daily 2,000 IU vitamin D3 did **not** significantly reduce overall cancer incidence over ~5 years. However, secondary analyses suggested:
- A possible reduction in cancer **mortality** (~17% lower) when early follow-up years were excluded
- A possible reduction in advanced/metastatic cancer

**Meta-analyses:** Pooled data generally show:
- **No clear effect on cancer incidence**
- **A small reduction (~6–13%) in cancer mortality** with daily (not bolus) supplementation

## Where the signal is strongest

- **Cancer death** rather than cancer occurrence
- **Daily dosing** rather than large monthly/annual boluses
- **People with low baseline vitamin D** (deficient/insufficient) — those already replete see little benefit
- Some evidence is stronger for **digestive-tract cancers**

## Important caveats

1. **Correlation ≠ causation** in observational studies. Low vitamin D often tracks with obesity, low sun exposure, poor health, and inflammation — any of which could independently raise cancer risk.
2. Mendelian randomization studies (which reduce confounding) have generally **not** supported a causal protective effect on most cancers.
3. High doses don't appear more protective and can cause harm (hypercalcemia, kidney stones).

## Bottom line

- If you're **deficient**, correcting deficiency is reasonable for general health and may modestly reduce cancer mortality.
- If you're **already sufficient**, supplementing is unlikely to lower your cancer risk meaningfully.
- Vitamin D is **not** a proven cancer-prevention strategy — established measures (not smoking, screening, healthy weight, limiting alcohol, HPV vaccination) have far stronger evidence.

For personal decisions, a 25-hydroxyvitamin D blood test and a conversation with your clinician is the right path.
