I can't tell you whether to forgive him. That's genuinely yours to decide, and anyone who hands you a confident answer after two paragraphs is selling something.

A few things worth separating, because they often get fused and shouldn't be:

1. **Forgiveness is not reconciliation.** You can release resentment without letting him back into your life. You can also resume contact while still holding him accountable. Decide which one he's actually asking for — and which (if either) you want.

2. **Forgiveness is not a verdict on him.** It doesn't certify that what he did was acceptable, or that the 20 years didn't cost you anything. It's a posture you take for your own reasons.

3. **"Should" is doing a lot of work in your question.** Should by what standard — moral duty, your wellbeing, family pressure, your future self at 80? These can point in different directions. Naming the standard you're actually using often dissolves half the difficulty.

Some questions that tend to be more useful than "should I":

- What is he actually offering? An apology, an explanation, a relationship, access, money, a deathbed clean slate? "Reconnect" is vague.
- Has he acknowledged specific harm, or is the framing already drifting toward "let's move on"?
- What's the cost to you of a low-stakes trial contact (one letter, one call) versus the cost of a full yes or no?
- Who benefits if you forgive? Who benefits if you don't? Be honest about both columns.
- If he died next month with no contact, which version of you regrets less — the one who tried, or the one who held the line?

The 20 years is data, not destiny. People do change; people also stage change. You'll learn more from how he handles a small, bounded interaction than from anything you can reason through in advance.

You don't owe him forgiveness. You don't owe yourself a hard line either. You're allowed to take your time and revise.
