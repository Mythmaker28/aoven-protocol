## Response (verbatim)

No, intermittent fasting is not proven to extend human lifespan in any rigorous sense [INTERPRET][CONF(high)]. The framing of "proven" itself collapses a gradient that the evidence does not warrant: lifespan extension in humans would require multi-decade randomized cohorts with all-cause mortality endpoints, and no such trial has been completed for any intermittent fasting (IF) protocol [FACT][CONF(high)] (data as of approximately mid-2024, per published reviews in JAMA, NEJM, and Cell Metabolism on time-restricted eating; [NOSRC] for exact citations in this turn, [LIMIT]).

What does exist is a layered evidence base of decreasing strength as you move from animals to humans [FACT][CONF(medium)]. Caloric restriction (not strictly IF) extends lifespan in yeast, worms, flies, and many rodent strains, though even in non-human primates the Wisconsin and NIA rhesus studies disagreed on mortality benefit, with diet composition appearing to matter as much as restriction itself [FACT][CONF(medium)] (data as of the 2017 Mattison et al. Nature Communications synthesis; [NOSRC] for full citation here). Human IF trials (TRE, alternate-day fasting, 5:2) have run on the order of weeks to ~12 months and report modest weight loss, some improvements in insulin sensitivity and lipids, with effects often comparable to ordinary caloric restriction once calories are matched [FACT][CONF(medium)] (data as of 2022–2024 RCT meta-analyses). None of these are powered for mortality [FACT][CONF(high)].

There are also signals worth flagging in the opposite direction. A 2024 American Heart Association EPI-Lifestyle conference abstract from a Shanghai group reported an association between an 8-hour TRE pattern and increased cardiovascular mortality in NHANES data [FACT][CONF(medium)] (data as of March 2024; abstract, not peer-reviewed full paper at announcement, [UNCERTAIN] regarding final published form). Observational, residual confounding plausible, but it illustrates that even the directional sign of long-term effects in free-living humans is contested [INTERPRET][CONF(medium)].

My intuition, separable from the evidence: the most likely truth is that IF is one of several roughly interchangeable tools for inducing modest energy deficit and metabolic flexibility in some people, and that any lifespan effect, if real, is small and mediated mostly through weight, glycemic control, and adherence rather than through a distinct fasting-specific longevity pathway [INTUIT][CONF(low-medium)]. This intuition cannot upgrade to [FACT] without a multi-decade hard-endpoint trial, which is unlikely to ever be funded at adequate scale [SPEC][CONF(medium)].

A hypothesis that would be falsifiable, if anyone ran it: a ≥10-year RCT of 16:8 TRE versus isocaloric unrestricted-window eating, ≥10,000 participants, primary endpoint all-cause mortality, would either show a hazard ratio whose 95% CI excludes 1.0 or it would not [HYP][CONF(high) on falsifiability, CONF(low) on it ever being executed]. Mechanistic surrogates (autophagy markers, mTOR signalling, ketone flux) are not adequate substitutes for that endpoint [INTERPRET][CONF(medium)].

Normatively, I think the responsible position is to treat IF as a reasonable dietary pattern for some metabolic goals where it suits a person's life, and to refuse the "proven longevity intervention" framing wherever it appears in popular media or supplement marketing [BELIEF][CONF(high)]. The honest summary: unproven for lifespan, modestly supported for short-term metabolic markers, with at least one non-trivial safety signal that has not yet been resolved [INTERPRET][CONF(medium)].
