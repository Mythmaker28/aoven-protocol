Short answer: For most people in wealthy countries in 2026, factory-farmed meat is hard to defend on any ethical framework that grants nontrivial weight to animal suffering. Other categories (subsistence, hunted game, well-regulated pasture systems, individuals with genuine medical constraints) are defensible to varying degrees. The blanket question hides a category error.

Breaking it down:

**Where the defense fails**
- ~70–80 billion land animals and ~1–2 trillion fish are killed per year, the overwhelming majority in conditions any honest observer would call cruel if inflicted on a dog.
- The traditional "necessity" defense (protein, B12, iron, complete amino acids) is empirically weak in 2026: precision-fermented and plant-based substitutes are nutritionally adequate, broadly available, and price-competitive in most OECD markets. "I prefer the taste/texture" is a preference claim, not a moral one.
- Climate/land-use externalities (~14–20% of anthropogenic GHGs, ~30% of habitable land) impose costs on third parties who didn't consent.
- The "animals lack moral status" move requires a coherent criterion (sentience, sapience, species membership) that either (a) excludes infants and the cognitively disabled, or (b) doesn't actually exclude pigs, cattle, and chickens.

**Where the defense survives**
- Subsistence contexts (Inuit, pastoralist communities, food-insecure regions) where alternatives are unavailable or culturally/ecologically destructive to impose.
- Hunted game from healthy populations: the animal had a wild life and one bad day; arguably better than the wild baseline of starvation/predation.
- Bivalves (mussels, oysters, clams): no plausible sentience, often net-positive for ecosystems.
- Genuine medical cases — narrow but real (e.g., severe allergen profiles, certain GI conditions). Self-reported "I feel better on meat" is weaker evidence than people treat it as.
- Pasture-raised ruminants on land that can't grow crops: defensible on land-use grounds, still has the killing problem.

**The honest cruxes**
1. How much moral weight do you assign to a chicken's suffering relative to a human's? If your answer is "zero," you owe an argument. If it's "nonzero," the math on 70 billion lives is brutal.
2. Are you willing to apply the same standard to your own consumption that you'd apply to a stranger's? Most meat-eaters' stated principles don't survive that test.

So: defensible in narrow cases, not defensible as the default Western diet. The interesting move in 2026 isn't whether to eat meat — it's whether the people who already think factory farming is wrong will act on it, or keep paying for it.
