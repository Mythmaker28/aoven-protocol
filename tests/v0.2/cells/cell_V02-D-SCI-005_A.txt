# Anti-Amyloid Antibodies in Early Alzheimer's Disease

**Short answer:** Yes, they show statistically significant effects on disease progression, but the clinical magnitude is modest and comes with meaningful safety trade-offs. Whether that constitutes "effective" depends on how you weight those factors.

## What the pivotal trials showed

**Lecanemab (Clarity AD, n≈1,795, 18 months)**
- Slowed decline on CDR-SB by **0.45 points** vs. placebo (27% relative reduction)
- Significant effects on secondary cognitive/functional measures (ADAS-Cog14, ADCS-MCI-ADL)
- Substantial amyloid PET clearance

**Donanemab (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, n≈1,736, 76 weeks)**
- Slowed decline on iADRS by ~**3 points** (~35% relative reduction in low/medium tau population)
- Slowed CDR-SB decline by ~0.7 points
- ~70% of low/medium tau participants achieved amyloid clearance

Both received FDA traditional approval (lecanemab 2023, donanemab 2024).

## Important caveats

1. **Clinical meaningfulness is debated.** A 0.45-point CDR-SB difference is below most proposed minimum clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds (~1.0–2.0 points). Patients and families may not perceive the change.

2. **ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) is common:**
 - Lecanemab: ~21% any ARIA (12.6% ARIA-E, 17.3% ARIA-H)
 - Donanemab: ~36% any ARIA (24% ARIA-E)
 - Symptomatic ARIA is less common but rare deaths have occurred, especially in **APOE ε4 homozygotes** (highest risk group).

3. **Narrow eligibility.** Benefit is established only in **MCI or mild dementia due to AD with confirmed amyloid pathology**. APOE genotyping is now recommended before treatment.

4. **Practical burden.** IV infusions (biweekly for lecanemab, monthly for donanemab), serial MRI monitoring, and high cost (~$26K–$32K/year before ancillary costs).

5. **Long-term and real-world effects** beyond 18 months remain uncertain; open-label extensions suggest sustained separation but no plateau in decline.

## Bottom line

These are the **first disease-modifying therapies** with replicated evidence of slowing AD progression — a genuine scientific milestone. But the effect size is small, ARIA risk is non-trivial (especially in ε4 homozygotes), and the eligible window is narrow. Reasonable clinicians disagree on whether the benefit-risk-burden balance favors routine use; shared decision-making with attention to APOE status is now standard.
