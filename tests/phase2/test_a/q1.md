## Response (verbatim)

Alzheimer's disease is a complex, progressive neurodegenerative disorder, and while researchers have learned a great deal about it, the exact cause is not fully understood. It likely results from a combination of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors that affect the brain over time.

At the biological level, Alzheimer's is characterized by two hallmark abnormalities in the brain. The first is the buildup of beta-amyloid protein into sticky plaques that accumulate between neurons, disrupting communication between brain cells. The second is the formation of neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons, made of a misfolded protein called tau. These tangles interfere with the internal transport system that neurons rely on to function. Together, these changes lead to widespread neuron death and brain atrophy, particularly in regions involved in memory like the hippocampus.

Genetics play a significant role. A small percentage of cases, called early-onset familial Alzheimer's, are caused by inherited mutations in genes such as APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2. For the more common late-onset form, the strongest known genetic risk factor is a variant of the APOE gene called APOE e4, though carrying it doesn't guarantee someone will develop the disease.

Age is by far the biggest risk factor. The likelihood of developing Alzheimer's roughly doubles every five years after age 65. Other risk factors include cardiovascular conditions like high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, and obesity, all of which can affect blood flow to the brain. A history of head injuries, chronic inflammation, poor sleep, depression, social isolation, low levels of physical and mental activity, smoking, and excessive alcohol use have also been linked to higher risk.

Researchers are exploring several theories about what triggers the cascade. The amyloid hypothesis suggests that beta-amyloid accumulation is the initiating event, while others point to tau pathology, neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, or even infectious agents as potential drivers. There's growing interest in the idea that Alzheimer's is not a single disease but rather a final common pathway resulting from multiple underlying processes.

Some risk factors are modifiable, which is encouraging. Regular physical exercise, a healthy diet such as the Mediterranean or MIND diet, staying socially and mentally engaged, getting quality sleep, managing cardiovascular health, and avoiding smoking are all associated with reduced risk. Estimates suggest that addressing modifiable risk factors could prevent or delay a meaningful percentage of dementia cases.

Despite ongoing research and recent advances in treatments that target amyloid plaques, there is currently no cure. Understanding the precise causes remains one of the most active areas of medical research, with the hope that clearer answers will lead to better prevention strategies and therapies.
