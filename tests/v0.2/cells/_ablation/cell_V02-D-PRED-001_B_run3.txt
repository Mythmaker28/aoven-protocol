Probably not, though "AGI" is contested enough that the answer hinges on definition.

**Against by 2030:**
- No current system generalizes robustly out-of-distribution; frontier LLMs still fail on tasks requiring reliable multi-step reasoning, persistent memory, and grounded world models.
- Sample efficiency, continual learning, and causal inference remain unsolved at the architectural level, not just at scale.
- Compute scaling is hitting power, fab, and data-availability constraints; the easy gains from pretraining-token scaling are largely spent.
- Robotics and embodied competence — usually included in any serious AGI definition — lag the language modality by a wide margin.
- Historical base rate: AI timelines have been systematically over-optimistic since the 1956 Dartmouth workshop.

**For by 2030:**
- Capability jumps from 2020→2025 were larger than most pre-2020 forecasters predicted; extrapolating naïvely puts something AGI-like in range.
- Agentic scaffolding, tool use, and inference-time compute (o-series, reasoning models) are unlocking capabilities not present in the base weights.
- Large labs are explicitly targeting 2027–2030 and allocating capital accordingly; self-fulfilling pressure exists.
- If "AGI" means "matches a competent remote knowledge worker on most economically valuable tasks" rather than "matches any human at any cognitive task," the bar is already partially cleared.

**My read:** Under a strict definition (human-level across cognitive domains, including robust embodied and scientific-discovery competence), no — likely 10–25% by Jan 1 2030. Under a weak definition (broadly competent agent that automates most desk work), maybe 35–55%. Anyone giving you a confident point estimate either side of those ranges is selling something.

The honest answer is that the question is poorly operationalized, and most public "AGI by 2030" claims collapse on inspection into either definitional sleight-of-hand or motivated forecasting.
