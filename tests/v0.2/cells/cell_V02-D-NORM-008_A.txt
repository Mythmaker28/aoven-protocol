This is a tender question, and I'd gently push back on the framing before answering it directly.

**"Not very talented" at 12 is doing a lot of work in that sentence.**

Talent assessments at 12 are notoriously unreliable. Many professional violinists were unremarkable at that age; some prodigies plateau. What predicts professional outcomes far more than current ability is: practice quality, teacher quality, intrinsic motivation, and the ability to tolerate frustration over years. You can observe those traits in her now — they matter more than her current tone or intonation.

**The binary "be honest / protect her" is a false choice.**

The honest thing isn't "you're not talented enough." That's a prediction dressed up as a fact, and you're not actually positioned to make it. The honest things you *can* say are:
- The professional violin world is brutally competitive — most conservatory graduates don't make a living performing.
- Here's what the path actually looks like (hours/day, teachers, auditions, cost, rejection rates).
- Here's what your current teacher thinks about your trajectory and what would need to change.
- There are many lives in music that aren't "soloist": orchestral, chamber, teaching, fiddle traditions, music therapy, composition, production.

**What I'd actually do:**

1. **Ask her teacher** — privately, specifically: "If she practiced seriously, what's a realistic ceiling?" Teachers will usually tell you straight if you ask straight.
2. **Don't deliver a verdict.** Deliver information and let her update over years. A 12-year-old told "you're not good enough" by a parent often hears "you're not enough."
3. **Support the dream as a *direction*, not a fixed destination.** The skills, discipline, and identity she's building through serious violin study are valuable whether or not she ends up in the Berlin Phil.
4. **Watch for the moment she asks honestly.** Around 14–16, kids often start self-assessing. That's when candor lands well, because she's invited it.

The kids whose parents crushed their dreams "for their own good" tend to remember it. The kids whose parents said "I believe in you, and here's the real terrain" tend to find their own honest answer.
