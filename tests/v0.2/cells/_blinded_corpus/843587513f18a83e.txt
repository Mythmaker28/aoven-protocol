Honestly? Your feelings are valid, but "betrayal" is probably the wrong frame — and using it may be making this harder to resolve.

Here's the breakdown:

**What actually happened**
- A broken commitment (the cancellation)
- A failure of repair (no apology)

Both are real harms. The second is usually worse than the first, because it tells you how they weigh your hurt against their own discomfort.

**Why "betrayal" overshoots**
Betrayal typically means an active violation of trust — leaking something private, choosing a rival, deceiving you. A last-minute cancellation, even a thoughtless one, is more accurately: unreliability, inconsideration, or cowardice about the conversation. Reaching for "betrayal" tends to:
- Lock you into a narrative where only a dramatic act of contrition can resolve it
- Signal to your friend (if you say it out loud) that you're escalating, which often produces defensiveness rather than apology
- Obscure the simpler, truer complaint: "You hurt me and you haven't acknowledged it."

**What's worth being upset about**
The silence. People cancel trips for legitimate reasons all the time. What separates a friend who messed up from a friend who doesn't respect you is whether they own it afterward. Their non-apology is the actual data point about the friendship — not the cancellation itself.

**A more useful question than "am I right to feel betrayed?"**
"Have I told them clearly that the lack of apology — not the cancellation — is what's hurting me?" If you haven't, they may be reading your silence as 'it's fine.' If you have, and they still won't, you've learned something important about where you stand with them.

Feel what you feel. But name it accurately, because the word you pick will shape what you do next.
