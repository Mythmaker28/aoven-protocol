## Response (verbatim)

Gene editing holds remarkable promise for treating and potentially eliminating many hereditary diseases, but completely eradicating all of them is a much taller order. The development of CRISPR-Cas9 and newer techniques like base editing and prime editing has already transformed what's possible. We've seen real successes, including the first FDA-approved CRISPR therapy, Casgevy, for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia, which directly addresses the genetic root cause rather than just managing symptoms.

For single-gene disorders with well-understood mutations, like Huntington's disease, cystic fibrosis, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and certain forms of inherited blindness, gene editing offers a clear therapeutic path. Many of these conditions could potentially be prevented or cured in affected individuals within the coming decades. Germline editing, which would alter eggs, sperm, or embryos, could theoretically prevent these diseases from being passed to future generations entirely.

However, several major obstacles stand in the way of total elimination. Many hereditary diseases are polygenic, involving complex interactions among hundreds or thousands of genetic variants alongside environmental factors. Conditions like schizophrenia, autism, and many cancers don't have single targets to edit. New mutations also arise spontaneously in every generation, meaning even if we eliminated existing disease-causing variants, fresh ones would continually appear.

There are also significant practical and ethical hurdles. Delivery remains challenging, as getting editing tools to the right cells in the body is harder than it sounds. Off-target effects and unintended consequences are still concerns. Germline editing in humans is banned or heavily restricted in most countries following the controversy around He Jiankui's edited babies in 2018, and there's broad scientific consensus that we're not ready to deploy it safely or ethically.

Access and equity are huge issues too. Current gene therapies cost millions of dollars per patient, putting them out of reach for most people who need them. Without dramatic cost reductions, any "elimination" of hereditary disease would only apply to wealthy populations.

The realistic outlook is that gene editing will progressively reduce the burden of hereditary diseases over the coming decades, especially monogenic ones, and will become a standard treatment option for many conditions. But complete elimination would require advances on multiple fronts: better understanding of complex genetic diseases, safe germline editing, universal screening and access, and sustained intervention across generations. It's more accurate to think of gene editing as a powerful tool that will dramatically shrink the toll of hereditary disease rather than a switch that flips it off entirely. The next twenty to thirty years should bring stunning progress, but "eliminate" is probably the wrong word for what's coming.
