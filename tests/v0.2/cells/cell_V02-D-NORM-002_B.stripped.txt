Short answer: probably not as a clean switch. Here's the honest read.

**What you're actually proposing**

"Full-time artist" usually means one of three very different things, and conflating them is the first mistake:
1. **Working artist** (gallery/commission/grant income) — extremely hard, low median income, long ramp.
2. **Commercial creative** (illustration, design, content) — viable, but it's a service business, not "being an artist."
3. **Self-funded artist** (you live off savings/spouse/passive income and make work) — totally fine if the math works.

Which one you mean changes the entire conversation. Most people considering this at 40 are imagining #1 but will end up doing #2.

**The uncomfortable parts**

- Median earnings for self-employed fine artists are low — typically well under what a finance career pays, and bimodal (a few do well, most don't).
- Skill alone doesn't sell work. The market rewards a specific bundle: distinctive voice, output volume, social presence, network, and persistence over 5–10 years. If you don't already have momentum on most of those at 40, you're starting a second career from zero.
- "I have savings" usually understates the cost. You're also giving up compounding, employer healthcare (US), and the optionality of finance income. Run the 20-year NPV, not just the 2-year burn rate.
- The romantic version ("I'll finally have time to make work") collides with the reality that running an art practice is ~30% making and ~70% admin, marketing, logistics, and rejection.

**What the strong version of this decision looks like**

People who pull this off generally have at least three of:
- A spouse/partner covering fixed costs, or 5+ years of expenses banked.
- An existing body of work and some external validation (sales, shows, following) before quitting.
- A clear product (not just "art") — a niche, a format, a buyer.
- Tolerance for income volatility they've actually tested, not just imagined.

**A more defensible path**

Don't quit. Run a 12–24 month parallel track: cut finance hours if possible, build the practice, get real market signal (sales, commissions, audience growth), and set a concrete go/no-go threshold *before* you start so you don't move the goalposts. If at month 18 you're hitting the threshold, leap. If not, you've learned something cheap instead of expensive.

**Bottom line**

The question isn't "is being an artist a good idea." It's "do I have evidence that *I* can be one full-time, or am I buying an identity?" At 40, in finance, with presumably no track record yet — the honest base rate says test before you jump.
