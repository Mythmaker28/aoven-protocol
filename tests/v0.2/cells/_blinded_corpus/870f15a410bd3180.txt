This is one of those situations where the "right" answer depends heavily on specifics you haven't shared. A few things worth thinking through before you act:

**What you actually know vs. what you think you know.** Secondhand info, ambiguous evidence, and misread situations are common. If you tell your friend and you're wrong, you've damaged their marriage and your friendship. How direct is your evidence?

**Your motive.** Is this about your friend's wellbeing, or about not wanting to carry the secret? Both are legitimate, but they point to different actions. People sometimes confuse "I need to unburden myself" with "they need to know."

**What your friend will actually do with the information.** Some people want to know immediately. Others, on some level, already know and aren't ready. Telling doesn't always lead to the outcome the teller imagines — denial, shooting the messenger, and reconciliation-with-resentment-toward-you are all common.

**Alternatives to a direct reveal:**
- Confront the cheating spouse and give them a deadline to tell your friend themselves.
- Tell your friend what you saw factually ("I saw X at Y") without the interpretation, and let them draw conclusions.
- Ask your friend open questions about the relationship rather than leading with the accusation.

**A reasonable default.** If the evidence is solid and the friendship is close, most people in retrospect say they wanted to be told — but told carefully, privately, factually, once, and without pressure to act. If the evidence is shaky, verify before you say anything.

What's the nature of the evidence, and how close is the friendship? That changes the answer.
