## Response (verbatim)

Economic inequality was a significant cause of the French Revolution, but it wasn't the only one, and historians generally see the Revolution as the result of multiple overlapping factors.

On the economic side, France in the late 1780s was in serious trouble. The state was effectively bankrupt, partly due to costly wars including support for the American Revolution, and partly due to an inefficient tax system. The burden of taxation fell heavily on the Third Estate (commoners), while the nobility and clergy enjoyed extensive exemptions despite owning much of the land and wealth. A series of poor harvests in the late 1780s, especially the disastrous 1788 harvest, drove bread prices sharply higher just as ordinary people could least afford it. Bread typically consumed half or more of a worker's wages, so this hit hard and fueled urban unrest.

But economic inequality alone doesn't explain why revolution happened then and there. Enlightenment ideas had reshaped how educated French people thought about government, rights, and legitimacy. Thinkers like Rousseau, Voltaire, and Montesquieu undermined traditional justifications for absolute monarchy and aristocratic privilege. The bourgeoisie, a rising and increasingly wealthy middle class, resented being shut out of political power and social status that the nobility monopolized. So the grievance wasn't just that some people were poor and others rich, but that the entire structure of privilege seemed irrational and unjust.

Political failures mattered enormously too. Louis XVI was indecisive and politically maladroit, and his attempts at reform were repeatedly blocked by the nobility and parlements protecting their privileges. When he finally convened the Estates-General in 1789 to address the financial crisis, the procedural disputes over voting opened the door for the Third Estate to assert itself as the National Assembly. Without that political miscalculation, the crisis might have unfolded differently.

There were also longer-term social changes: population growth, the spread of literacy, an expanding public sphere with newspapers and pamphlets, and the example of the American Revolution showing that monarchies could be overthrown.

So the most defensible answer is that economic inequality, including unfair taxation and immediate hardship from food prices, created the combustible conditions, but Enlightenment ideology, the ambitions of the bourgeoisie, the bankruptcy of the state, and the political mistakes of the monarchy were what actually ignited and shaped the Revolution. Historians have argued for centuries about which factor mattered most. Marxist historians traditionally emphasized class and economics, while revisionists since the 1960s have stressed political culture and ideology. The current consensus tends to integrate both, treating the Revolution as a confluence rather than the product of any single cause.
